A prospect just asked if you're SOC 2 compliant. Or you heard at a founder event that you can't close enterprise deals without it. Now you're wondering if you should start.

Here is the rule we give founders, including the ones we tell not to buy from us yet: **don't start SOC 2 until a customer asks for it in writing.** When they do, start the same day.

The rest of this page is how to tell the difference, and what to do in each case.

## Key takeaways

- **Nobody needs SOC 2 by law.** You need it when a customer makes it a condition.
- **Wait for a request in writing, with a name and a date.** "We should probably have it" is not a trigger.
- **When the request comes, put the date in the customer contract.** Commit to four months, start the day they sign, and pay after the revenue.
- **Before anyone asks, keep the cheap things ready.** Policies, a security page, and answers to the usual questionnaire.
- **It costs about 5 to 10 hours of your time** when someone does the work for you.

## Who needs a SOC 2 report?

SOC 2 is a report on how you protect your customers' data, written by a licensed CPA firm. People call it "SOC 2 certification", but it is an attestation report, not a certificate (here is [what SOC 2 actually is](/blog/2025-10-28-what-is-soc2)).

Nobody is legally required to have one. The companies that end up needing it look like this:

- they sell to other businesses,
- they store or process those customers' data,
- and at least some of those customers are in the US.

That covers most B2B SaaS, AI, data and infrastructure startups. It does not mean you need it today.

If your buyers are banks, insurers or European companies, they will more likely ask for ISO 27001. Check before you commit to anything: [SOC 2 vs ISO 27001](/blog/2025-10-08-soc2-or-iso27001) explains how to ask.

## Six signs it is time

Every company we have seen start SOC 2 for a good reason had one of these, with a date attached.

1. **A named customer asked in writing.** An email, a contract clause, a procurement form. Not a comment on a call.
2. **A security questionnaire is blocking a signature right now.** The deal is agreed and legal or security will not release it.
3. **A regulation or a licence has a deadline.** HIPAA for a US health customer, NIS2, DORA, a payment licence. Check which framework it really requires. It is often not SOC 2.
4. **An investor, partner or board asked for it in due diligence.**
5. **You are entering an accelerator batch or a demo day** where your first enterprise pilots will ask within weeks.
6. **Your current compliance tool is up for renewal and the project has stalled.** You are already paying. The question is how to finish.

If none of these is true, you are early. That is fine, and it is the cheaper position to be in.

## When it is too early

The most common mistake is treating [SOC 2](/hub/soc2) as something every serious startup does.

What worked for another founder may be irrelevant to you. A company selling to US enterprises has a different calculation than a European startup selling to mid-market, or a fintech with its own regulator.

Before copying what you heard on stage, answer two questions. Who exactly is asking me for this? What would I sign next week if I had it?

If the honest answers are "nobody" and "nothing", do not start. You would spend real money and three or four months of attention on a report that expires after a year and that nobody has asked to read.

## What to have before anyone asks

Being early does not mean doing nothing. A few cheap things make the first request easy to answer:

- **Policies that describe what you really do.** Access, onboarding and offboarding, incidents, backups, vendors.
- **A public security page.** Many prospects who ask about security are not auditors. They need to tick a box internally. A clear [compliance portal](/products/compliance-portal) that explains your controls and infrastructure answers most of those questions without an audit.
- **Answers to the standard questionnaire,** written once and reused.
- **The basics switched on.** Single sign-on, multi-factor authentication, disk encryption on laptops, access removed the day someone leaves.

## What to do the day a customer asks

This is the part most founders get backwards. They start the SOC 2 project, spend three months and the budget, and then find out the prospect chose someone else.

Do it in this order instead.

**1. Get the request in writing.** Ask which report they need ([Type 1 or Type 2](/hub/soc2-type1-vs-type2), and whether ISO 27001 would also be accepted) and by when.

**2. Put the date in the customer contract.** Commit to deliver the SOC 2 report within four months of signature. A serious buyer accepts this. Enterprise customers rarely need the report on day one. They need to know it is coming.

**3. Start the day they sign.** Sign with your compliance provider the same day. The auditor is booked within a week and the observation period starts.

**4. Show proof while the audit runs.** An engagement letter from the auditor with the audit dates, and an honest "SOC 2 in progress" line on your security page. Here is [how to announce SOC 2 in progress](/blog/2026-09-17-how-to-announce-soc-2-in-progress) without overclaiming.

**5. Pay after the revenue.** The cost lands after the contract that justifies it, not before.

We did this for our own certification. A customer asked us in May, we committed to three months in the contract, and we delivered.

If the buyer will not put anything in writing, that tells you how real the deal is. It is better to learn that before the audit than after.

## What SOC 2 will and will not do for you

It will unblock the deal that asked for it, and the next ones like it.

It will shorten security questionnaires. With a current Type 2 report, many standard questions are already answered.

It will not make questionnaires disappear. Large companies run their own vendor reviews and will still ask about subprocessors, data retention and incident response.

SOC 2 is a foundation, not a firewall.

It is also only as good as the firm that signs it. Before you pay for an audit, take 20 minutes to [check the auditor](/hub/how-to-check-your-auditor).

## How much of your time it takes

Two numbers matter more than the invoice.

If you run SOC 2 yourself with a tool, plan 40 to 100 hours of founder or engineer time.

If a compliance officer does the work for you, plan 5 to 10 hours of your CTO's time over the whole project: the onboarding call, a few access and configuration changes, and the evidence call with the auditor.

For the money, see [what SOC 2 costs in 2026](/blog/2025-03-04-what-is-soc2-cost). The short version for a startup under 15 people is about $15,000 in year one, audit included.

## The bottom line

Compliance is a business decision, not a social one. Wait for a customer to ask in writing. When they do, put the date in their contract, start the same day, and let the revenue pay for the report.
